# Scales and modes in electronic music

Research for the harmony learning tool's shortlist. Five-minute read.

## Summary

The confident claim — natural minor (Aeolian) dominates electronic dance
music broadly, with Phrygian and Phrygian-flavored sounds as the main
"exotic" accent — holds up. What's weaker than the internet suggests: how
much of that "exotic" sound is actually **harmonic minor** or true **Phrygian
dominant**, as opposed to natural minor with one borrowed note. That specific
claim is repeated constantly on production forums and rarely demonstrated
against real tracks. Treat sections 1 and 5 below as the load-bearing ones.

## 1. What's actually dominant, by subgenre

**Across EDM generally:** minor keys outnumber major. The one academic
corpus study found in this research — key estimation work on an EDM
dataset — found F minor as the single most frequent key, followed closely by
C minor and G minor, and noted that EDM's key distribution is more evenly
spread across all 12 chromatic tones than in other genres, plausibly because
producers work from synthesizers/DAWs rather than fixed instrument tunings
that favor certain keys ([Six, Cornelis & Leman, "Key Estimation in
Electronic Dance Music," ISMIR proceedings via
Springer](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-3-319-30671-1_25)).
This is the strongest evidence-based source found; treat everything below it
as corroborating but weaker (blogs, forums, single-artist stat pages).

- **Techno / hard techno:** predominantly **natural minor (Aeolian)**,
  harmonically static — one- or two-chord loops, or no chords at all, with
  movement carried by a bassline or filter sweep rather than a progression.
  **Phrygian** (and its flat-second color specifically) shows up as the genre's
  signature "dark" accent, especially in hard techno and darker peak-time
  techno ([Loopmasters, "Music Theory Tips Part 4: Advanced
  Modes"](https://www.loopmasters.com/articles/3509-Music-Theory-Tips-Part-4-Advanced-Modes);
  [Splice, "The Phrygian mode: a comprehensive
  guide"](https://splice.com/blog/music-modes-phrygian/)). A per-artist stats
  page (auto-generated, not a rigorous source) puts Adam Beyer's catalog at
  roughly an even 50/50 major/minor split across 101 tracks — a useful
  reminder that "techno is minor" is a strong tendency, not a rule
  ([mixgraph.io, Adam Beyer profile](https://www.mixgraph.io/artists/adam-beyer)).
- **House (deep house, tech house):** minor keys dominate, often colored
  with jazz-derived 7th/9th extensions rather than triads — this is what
  gives deep house its "moody but smooth" character rather than techno's
  starker minor ([Attack Magazine, "Deep House
  Chords"](https://www.attackmagazine.com/technique/passing-notes/passing-notes-deep-house-chords/);
  [Mixed In Key, "House Music
  Chords"](https://mixedinkey.com/captain-plugins/wiki/house-music-chords/)).
  Main-room / big-room house leans on large, emotionally broad minor
  progressions ([Attack Magazine, "Main Room House Chord
  Progressions"](https://www.attackmagazine.com/technique/passing-notes/main-room-house-chord-progressions/)).
- **Trance (uplifting/anthem trance):** natural minor is the base, very
  frequently colored by borrowing the raised 7th from **harmonic minor**
  for the "anthemic" lift in melodies and to enable a major/dominant V or
  VII chord — e.g., in A minor, borrowing G♯ to get an E major or E7 chord
  ([Theory Helper, "Best Scales for Trance
  Music"](https://www.theoryhelper.com/genres/trance/scales); corroborated
  by forum discussion at
  [TranceAddict](https://tranceaddict.com/forums/printthread.php?s=fec0542569ec928a7e203f2602d7f786&threadid=534439)).
  This borrowed-7th pattern is well attested and more precise than a blanket
  "trance uses harmonic minor" claim — most of the scale stays natural minor;
  only the leading tone changes.
- **Drum and bass (liquid):** minor scales dominate; **F minor** is
  frequently cited as the most common key, partly for sub-bass/system reasons
  (frequencies around F0 translate well on club systems), with harmonic
  minor also common for a "neo-classical" liquid feel. The rest of DnB
  (neurofunk, jump-up) is described as more chromatic/atonal, built around
  sound design rather than scale ([Dogs on Acid forum
  thread](https://www.dogsonacid.com/threads/best-key-s-for-writing-drum-n-bass.691833/);
  [EDMProd, "How To Make Liquid Drum & Bass"](https://www.edmprod.com/how-to-make-liquid-drum-and-bass/)).
  This is forum consensus, not measured corpus data — flag it as such.
- **Hard dance / hardstyle:** natural minor (Aeolian) is the stated base,
  but sources are explicit that hardstyle uses noticeably more **major**
  keys than most other electronic genres, and mixes harmonic minor in for
  melodic tension. The claim that hard dance is essentially harmonic-minor
  territory is an overstatement — Aeolian and major both show up heavily
  ([Harderstate forum, "Easy music theory (scales, chords and
  stuff)"](https://harderstate.com/tutorials-f22/easy-music-theory-scales-chords-and-stuff-t2633.html)).
- **Psytrance:** this is where Phrygian and **Phrygian dominant** are most
  legitimately central, especially in the flat-second riffs and arpeggios
  used in intros and lead lines, giving the genre's "psychedelic," Middle
  Eastern-tinged drive ([Outerverse.fm, "Understanding Scales & Modes in
  Psytrance"](https://outerverse.fm/blogs/tutorials/understanding-scales-modes-in-psytrance)).
  Still, most of a psytrance track's bassline/groove sits on a plain minor
  or even a single pitch-class drone; Phrygian dominant is a lead/arp
  device layered on top, not the harmonic foundation of the whole track.
- **Ambient:** the outlier. Brian Eno's foundational ambient work uses
  slow-moving drones and pads without functional harmony at all; when a
  mode is identifiable, **Lydian** (raised 4th, "floating" quality) and
  **Mixolydian** are cited as characteristic, alongside Dorian for a more
  melancholic-but-not-heavy color
  ([ModeAudio, "Ambient Pioneers: Brian
  Eno"](https://modeaudio.com/magazine/ambient-pioneers-brian-eno);
  [Reverb Machine, "Deconstructing Brian Eno's Music for
  Airports"](https://reverbmachine.com/blog/deconstructing-brian-eno-music-for-airports/)).
  This is the one subgenre here where minor/Aeolian is not the default.
- **Synthwave:** minor keys dominate overall (A minor is cited as the most
  common single key across a 50-song sample), but **Dorian** is called out
  repeatedly as the genre's characteristic mode — natural minor with a
  raised 6th — because it signals "retro" without tipping into straight
  melancholy ([Orpheus Audio Academy, "What Key Is Synthwave Music In? (50
  Songs Examined)"](https://www.orpheusaudioacademy.com/keysynthwave/);
  [Synthwave Pro, "Modes and Scales in Synthwave
  Music"](https://synthwavepro.com/modes-and-scales-in-synthwave-music/)).
  The 50-song sample is a hobbyist analysis, not peer-reviewed, but it's the
  most concrete number available for this subgenre.

**Bottom line on section 1:** natural minor is the load-bearing scale
everywhere except ambient. Phrygian is real and specific to darker
techno/psytrance, not a genre-wide EDM feature. Dorian is real and specific
to synthwave (and much of house/liquid DnB's "brighter minor" moments).
Lydian/Mixolydian are real but essentially confined to ambient.

## 2. What gives each scale its character

- **Natural minor (Aeolian).** Formula: 1–2–♭3–4–5–♭6–♭7. The defining
  interval is the **flat 3rd** (makes it "minor" at all) combined with a
  **flat 6th and flat 7th** that keep both the chord built on the 5th degree
  and the chord built on the 4th degree minor too — there's no strong pull
  back to "home," so it can loop indefinitely without needing to resolve.
  In plain terms: it sounds serious or melancholic, but not tense — it's
  comfortable being sad, which is exactly why it can loop for eight minutes
  without wearing out its welcome
  ([Wikipedia, "Aeolian
  mode"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aeolian_mode); [Gearspace, "Aeolian
  Mode in dance music"](https://gearspace.com/board/electronic-music-instruments-and-electronic-music-production/1020966-quick-music-theory-aeolian-mode-dance-music.html)).
- **Phrygian.** Formula: 1–♭2–♭3–4–5–♭6–♭7. The defining interval is the
  **flat 2nd** — the note right next to the root, one semitone up, instead
  of the usual two. That half-step sits right on top of the root and drags
  the ear back down to it. In plain terms: it sounds dark, tense, and
  vaguely "foreign" or unsettled — the sound of unease rather than sadness
  ([Wikipedia, "Phrygian
  mode"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phrygian_mode); [Splice, "The
  Phrygian mode"](https://splice.com/blog/music-modes-phrygian/)).
- **Dorian.** Formula: 1–2–♭3–4–5–6–♭7. It's minor (flat 3rd) but keeps a
  **natural (not flat) 6th** — the one note that natural minor flattens.
  That single restored note stops the scale from tipping into full sadness.
  In plain terms: it sounds minor but not heavy — wistful, cool, a little
  hopeful, which is why it reads as "retro" rather than "sad"
  ([Wikipedia via LANDR, "Modes
  Explained"](https://blog.landr.com/music-modes/); [Synthwave Pro, "Modes
  and Scales in Synthwave Music"](https://synthwavepro.com/modes-and-scales-in-synthwave-music/)).
- **Harmonic minor.** Formula: 1–2–♭3–4–5–♭6–7 — natural minor with the 7th
  degree raised back to normal (not flattened). The defining interval is
  the **augmented 2nd gap** this creates between the flat 6th and the raised
  7th — a stretched, wider-than-usual step. In plain terms: it sounds
  minor and serious like natural minor, but with a snap of tension right
  before it resolves — the note that "wants" to pull home more urgently
  ([Wikipedia via Learn Jazz Standards, "Phrygian Dominant
  Scale"](https://www.learnjazzstandards.com/blog/phrygian-dominant-scale/)
  covers this relationship directly).
- **Phrygian dominant.** Formula: 1–♭2–3–4–5–♭6–♭7 — Phrygian's flat 2nd,
  but with a normal (not flat) 3rd. The defining interval is that same
  **flat 2nd**, now combined with a major 3rd, which creates the same
  stretched augmented-2nd gap as harmonic minor (it's the 5th mode of
  harmonic minor). In plain terms: it sounds exotic and dramatic —
  simultaneously dark (from the flat 2nd) and bright/aggressive (from the
  major 3rd), which is why it reads as "Spanish" or "Middle Eastern" to
  Western ears ([Wikipedia, "Phrygian dominant
  scale"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phrygian_dominant_scale)).
- **Lydian** (ambient only, minor share of the shortlist relevance).
  Formula: 1–2–3–♯4–5–6–7 — a major scale with the 4th raised. The defining
  interval is that **raised 4th**, the only note different from an ordinary
  major scale. In plain terms: it sounds bright but slightly unmoored — not
  quite resolved, which reads as "floating" or "dreamlike" rather than
  triumphant ([ModeAudio, "Ambient Pioneers: Brian
  Eno"](https://modeaudio.com/magazine/ambient-pioneers-brian-eno)).

## 4. Characteristic chord progressions

- **Natural minor (techno / house / trance base):**
  **i–VI–VII** and **i–VI–III–VII** are the two workhorses — described
  repeatedly as *the* minor-key loop for techno and tech house because they
  cycle without ever needing a dominant resolution
  ([Samplesound, "Popular Chord Progressions in EDM, Techno, and
  More"](https://www.samplesoundmusic.com/blogs/news/popular-chord-progressions-and-how-to-use-them-in-edm-techno-and-more)).
  Degrees used: i, III, VI, VII (all naturally minor/major within the
  scale — no altered notes needed). Degree avoided: **v** — natural minor's
  5-chord is minor, which gives a weak, non-pulling resolution back to i, so
  producers route around it entirely rather than lean on it
  ([Musiversal, "Master The Aeolian
  Mode"](https://musiversal.com/blog/master-aeolian-mode)). The related
  **i–VII–VI–VII** ("Andalusian-adjacent" but ending on VII, not V) is
  documented as a broader pop/rock pattern that electronic genres share
  ([Puget Sound Music Theory, "The i–VII–VI–VII
  Progression"](https://musictheory.pugetsound.edu/mt21c/i-VII-VI-VII.html)).
- **Harmonic-minor-tinged trance:** **vi–IV–I–V** (viewed from the relative
  major) or equivalently **i–VI–III–V** in the minor, where the V is made
  major/dominant by borrowing the raised 7th. This is the "anthem lift"
  progression — it's the one place electronic music commonly reaches for a
  real dominant resolution, and it's specifically associated with the
  emotional "drop is coming" build in trance
  ([Unison Audio, "9 Trance Chord
  Progressions"](https://unison.audio/trance-chord-progressions/)).
- **Deep house:** minor triads extended to **m7 / m9** chords over the same
  i–VI–VII-family loops, rarely a full ii–V–I jazz cadence — the extensions
  add color without adding functional pull
  ([Attack Magazine, "Deep House
  Chords"](https://www.attackmagazine.com/technique/passing-notes/passing-notes-deep-house-chords/)).
- **Techno / minimal:** frequently **no chord progression at all** — a
  single pedal note, a two-chord vamp (i–VII or i–VI), or harmony implied
  by a moving bassline under static pads. This is a genuine and important
  point of difference from house/trance: techno's "harmony" is often just
  one sustained tonal center
  ([source discussing minimal techno's harmonic minimalism](https://technospacetrip.com/minimal-techno/)).
- **Phrygian / hard techno:** progressions are rare by design — Phrygian
  color usually appears as a **riff or bassline outlining i and ♭II**
  (root and flat-2), not as a chord progression in the classical sense,
  since the genre is largely monophonic/percussive at the harmonic level
  ([Splice, "The Phrygian mode"](https://splice.com/blog/music-modes-phrygian/)).

## 5. Harmonic minor and Phrygian dominant: how real is the "everywhere in hard dance/psytrance" claim?

**Verdict: overstated as stated, real in a narrower form.**

- The claim survives in a specific, narrower shape: Phrygian dominant is
  genuinely characteristic of **psytrance lead/arp lines and intros**, where
  its flat-2-plus-major-3rd combination produces the genre's signature
  "psychedelic" and Middle-Eastern-adjacent sound
  ([Outerverse.fm](https://outerverse.fm/blogs/tutorials/understanding-scales-modes-in-psytrance)).
  That source is a production-education blog, not an academic or
  journalistic one, so treat it as informed practitioner opinion rather
  than settled fact.
- It does **not** survive as a claim about the harmonic *foundation* of
  psytrance or hard dance tracks as a whole. Psytrance basslines and grooves
  are overwhelmingly built on a static root note or plain minor scale;
  Phrygian dominant appears as a melodic/lead layer on top, not as the
  scale the whole track sits in.
- For hardstyle/hard dance specifically, sources explicitly push back on a
  harmonic-minor-only picture: hardstyle uses **more major-key material**
  than most electronic genres, and harmonic minor is one ingredient mixed
  with natural minor and major, not a dominant default
  ([Harderstate forum thread](https://harderstate.com/tutorials-f22/easy-music-theory-scales-chords-and-stuff-t2633.html)).
  This is forum-sourced, not authoritative, but it directly contradicts the
  stronger "hard dance = harmonic minor" folk claim, which makes it useful
  as a check.
- No source found in this research offers a corpus-level statistic (e.g.,
  "X% of psytrance tracks use Phrygian dominant"). Every claim about
  harmonic minor/Phrygian dominant prevalence in this research traces back
  to production-blog or forum-level assertion, not measurement. Compare
  this to the natural-minor claim in section 1, which does have a corpus
  study behind it. This asymmetry is the clearest finding of this research:
  **natural minor's dominance is evidence-based; harmonic minor / Phrygian
  dominant's dominance in hard dance and psytrance is repeated folk wisdom,
  plausible and directionally consistent with what production blogs
  describe, but not independently verified here.**

## Recommendation: shortlist for the learning tool

Ranked by how load-bearing each scale is across electronic music as a
whole, balanced against how distinct and teachable its character is.

1. **Natural minor (Aeolian)** — the default scale of electronic music
   generally; without it the tool has no baseline to contrast anything
   against.
2. **Phrygian** — the most common and most clearly evidenced "second
   scale" in electronic music, distinct enough (one flipped note, huge mood
   change) to teach the flat-2 concept cleanly.
3. **Dorian** — genuinely characteristic of a named subgenre (synthwave)
   and of house/liquid DnB's "bright minor" moments; teaches the
   raised-6th concept as a direct, minimal contrast to natural minor.
4. **Harmonic minor** — real and specific (the trance "borrowed leading
   tone" move), and it's the natural bridge to Phrygian dominant and to
   understanding why some minor-key tracks suddenly get a major V chord.
5. **Phrygian dominant** — belongs on the list for its distinct, memorable
   sound and its clear relationship to harmonic minor (5th mode), but
   should be framed honestly as a lead/melodic device in psytrance rather
   than a harmonic foundation — the tool should not imply it structures
   whole tracks the way natural minor does.
6. **Lydian** — optional sixth slot. Only earns a place if the tool wants
   to cover ambient; it's the one scale here that is not minor-family and
   would demonstrate that electronic music isn't monolithically minor. Cut
   it first if the shortlist needs to shrink to five.
